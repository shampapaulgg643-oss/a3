The IPA sound guide for English

Every vowel and consonant sound of General American English, with example words, simple descriptions and a quick explanation of how the International Phonetic Alphabet works.

What is the IPA?

One symbol, one sound

The International Phonetic Alphabet was created in the late nineteenth century so that linguists and teachers could write down the sounds of any language accurately. In the IPA, each symbol always stands for the same sound, no matter which word it appears in.

Symbols are written between slashes, like /ʃ/, when we mean a meaningful sound in a language. A colon-like mark, /ː/, shows that a vowel is long, and the mark /ˈ/ comes before a stressed syllable.

You don’t need to memorise the whole chart. The 40 or so symbols below cover everything you need for English.

Vowels

Vowel sounds

Vowels are made with an open vocal tract. What changes is the height and position of your tongue, the shape of your lips and the length of the sound.

SymbolExampleHow to make it
/iː/Long and tense. Spread your lips slightly, as if smiling, with the tongue high and forward.
/ɪ/Short and relaxed. The tongue is a little lower and further back than for /iː/.
/eɪ/A glide: start near /e/ and move toward /ɪ/. Your jaw closes slightly as you finish.
/ɛ/Short, with the mouth half open and the tongue front and mid-height.
/æ/Open the jaw wide and keep the tongue low and forward. Common in American English.
/ɑː/Long and open, with the tongue low and back and the jaw dropped.
/ɔː/Rounded lips, tongue low and back. Many American speakers merge it with /ɑː/.
/oʊ/A glide from a rounded /o/ toward /ʊ/. The lips round more as you finish.
/ʊ/Short and relaxed, with lightly rounded lips and the tongue high and back.
/uː/Long, with tightly rounded lips and the tongue high and back.
/ʌ/Short and central, with a relaxed, slightly open mouth. Stressed syllables only.
/ə/The schwa. The most common vowel in English, found in unstressed syllables. Very short and relaxed.
/ɜːr/A central vowel coloured by /r/. The tongue bunches or curls slightly back.
/aɪ/Start with an open /a/ and glide up toward /ɪ/.
/aʊ/Start open and glide toward a rounded /ʊ/. The lips close and round.
/ɔɪ/Start with rounded /ɔ/ and glide toward /ɪ/, unrounding the lips.
Consonants

Quick tips

Reading IPA in a dictionary

  • Check the accent. Many dictionaries show both British and American forms. Choose one and stay consistent.
  • Find the stress mark. The symbol /ˈ/ comes before the stressed syllable: /ˈwɔːtər/ is WA-ter.
  • Notice the schwa. /ə/ appears in most unstressed syllables. Keep it short and relaxed.
  • Voiced or voiceless? Place a finger on your throat. If you feel a buzz, the sound is voiced.
  • Say it, don’t just read it. Always speak the word aloud after checking its transcription.
